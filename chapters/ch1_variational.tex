\framepart{Outline}
This chapter develops a single idea: a wide class of elliptic boundary value
problems can be recast as variational problems, and this reformulation is not
a cosmetic convenience. It is the natural entry point into existence theory,
numerical methods and convex duality. The chapter starts from a discrete
structure, a chain of springs, where everything reduces to linear algebra.
It then follows one running example, the Poisson problem $-\Delta u=f$,
extended progressively to general elliptic operators, nonlinear energies,
constraints and finite elements.

\paragraph{By the end of this chapter you should be able to:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item derive the weak form of a linear elliptic PDE from its strong form,
  and conversely, using the integration-by-parts formulae of
  Appendix~\ref{app:ibp};
  \item state and apply the direct method of the calculus of variations and
  the Lax--Milgram theorem to establish existence and uniqueness;
  \item construct the dual (complementary-energy) problem with the
  Legendre--Fenchel transform, and treat constraints with Lagrange
  multipliers;
  \item recognize the structure $\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}$ underlying springs,
  circuits, trusses and finite elements;
  \item recognize the structure of adjoint operators in the Tonti diagram, and
  make the precise link between differential operators and their
  linear-algebra counterparts: gradient and $\mat{B}$, divergence and
  $-\mat{B}\T$, bilinear form and stiffness matrix;
  \item know the function spaces in which the energy is finite, with their
  examples and counterexamples;
  \item assemble a finite element stiffness matrix, state C\'ea's lemma, and
  explain how the B-spline bases of isogeometric analysis differ from finite
  elements in Ciarlet's sense;
  \item derive a strong PDE from an energy, reconstruct an energy from a
  symmetric weak form, and recognize when no energy exists.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Plan of the chapter.} Section~\ref{sec:var-intro} explains why the question
matters. Section~\ref{sec:var-discrete} works out the discrete picture:
springs, the Tonti diagram, the minimum of the energy, symmetry,
reciprocity, and constraints with Lagrange multipliers.
Section~\ref{sec:var-spaces} introduces the function spaces of the continuum.
Sections~\ref{sec:var-weak}--\ref{sec:var-elliptic} pass to the
continuum: weak and strong forms, existence by minimization, the
Lax--Milgram theorem and general elliptic operators.
Section~\ref{sec:var-dual} constructs the dual problem,
Section~\ref{sec:var-fem} discretizes by finite elements and by isogeometric
analysis, and Section~\ref{sec:var-phys} returns to
physics with minimal surfaces. A summary, exercises with solutions
(page~\pageref{sec:var-exo}, starting with short exam-style questions) and the
references close the chapter. The technical definitions of the variational
derivatives (G\^ateaux, Fr\'echet, functional derivative) are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:derivatives}.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:var-intro}
\index{variational formulation}\index{weak form}\index{Euler--Lagrange equation}\index{Tonti diagram}%
Variational formulations translate differential or operator equations into
problems of minimizing, or finding critical points of, a functional over a
well-chosen function space. This translation unlocks the tools of functional
analysis: compactness, convexity, duality and fixed-point
arguments~\cite{Evans2010,Brezis2011,Dacorogna2008}. In practice it allows us
\begin{itemize}
  \item to construct the \emph{weak form} of a PDE, which is what finite
  elements discretize;
  \item to derive a PDE from an energy: the \emph{Euler--Lagrange equation};
  \item to recognize the dual kinematic/static structure, the \emph{Tonti
  diagram}, underlying all elliptic equations;
  \item to understand why the basic properties (symmetry, positivity,
  boundary conditions) matter, and how the continuous and discrete forms
  correspond.
\end{itemize}
Integration by parts, in the form of the Gauss--Ostrogradsky (divergence)
theorem, is used throughout; the whole family of identities is collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:ibp}. The model problem of the chapter is

\begin{gbox}{Model problem}
\index{Poisson problem}\index{energy!Dirichlet}%
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson}
  -\Delta u = f \ \text{in } \Omega, \qquad u = 0 \ \text{on } \partial\Omega,
  \qquad f \in L^2(\Omega),
\end{equation}
with $\Omega\subset\R^n$ a bounded Lipschitz domain. Its energy is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson-energy}
  J(v) = \frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla v|^2\dd x - \int_\Omega f v\dd x,
  \qquad v\in V=H^1_0(\Omega).
\end{equation}
\end{gbox}

\section{A discrete prelude: springs and the matrix \texorpdfstring{$\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}$}{BtCB}}\label{sec:var-discrete}
Gilbert Strang's \emph{Introduction to Applied Mathematics}~\cite{Strang1986}
organizes a large range of equilibrium problems around a single diagram. It
is the discrete skeleton of every continuum problem of this book, and the
place where the main ideas can be seen with linear algebra alone.

\subsection{Positive definiteness of \texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$}{AtA} and \texorpdfstring{$\mat{A}\T\mat{C}\mat{A}$}{AtCA}}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:AtCA}
\index{positive definiteness!of AtCA@of $\mat{A}\T\mat{C}\mat{A}$}\index{least squares}%
Let $\mat{A}$ be an $m\times n$ matrix ($n\le m$) with independent columns.
Then $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ is symmetric positive definite. More generally, if
$\mat{C}$ is symmetric positive definite, so is $\mat{A}\T\mat{C}\mat{A}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any $\vx\in\R^n$, $\vx\T\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vx=|\mat{A}\vx|^2\ge0$, and
$|\mat{A}\vx|=0$ implies $\mat{A}\vx=\vzero$, hence $\vx=\vzero$ since the
columns of $\mat{A}$ are independent. Symmetry is immediate. In the general
case, with $|\vect{y}|_{\mat{C}}^2=\vect{y}\T\mat{C}\vect{y}$,
$\vx\T\mat{A}\T\mat{C}\mat{A}\vx=|\mat{A}\vx|_{\mat{C}}^2$, which is a norm
because $\mat{C}>0$; the same argument applies.
\end{proof}
For $\mat{A}\vx=\vb$, the normal equations $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}\vx=\mat{A}\T\vb$
are the equations of least squares; $\mat{A}\T\mat{A}$ can be analysed with
the singular value decomposition of $\mat{A}$. Note that $\mat{A}$ need not
be invertible, nor even square: only the independence of its columns is
required.

Let $\vu=(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ be the nodal displacements, $\ve=(e_1,\dots,e_4)$ the
spring elongations, $\vect{\sigma}=(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_4)$ the spring
forces and $\vf$ the external nodal forces. The three groups of equations are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{kinematics} (compatibility), $\ve=\mat{B}\vu$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} e_1\\ e_2\\ e_3\\ e_4 \end{bmatrix}
  =\begin{bmatrix} 1 & & \\ -1 & 1 & \\ & -1 & 1 \\ & & -1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ u_3 \end{bmatrix};
\]
\item \emph{constitutive law} (Hooke), $\vect{\sigma}=\mat{C}\ve$, with
$\mat{C}=\diag(c_1,c_2,c_3,c_4)$ the spring stiffnesses, all positive,
$c_i>0$, so that $\mat{C}$ is positive definite;
\item \emph{equilibrium} of each mass, $\vf=\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}$:
\[
  \begin{bmatrix} f_1 \\ f_2 \\ f_3 \end{bmatrix}
  =\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & & \\ & 1 & -1 & \\ & & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1\\ \sigma_2\\ \sigma_3\\ \sigma_4 \end{bmatrix}.
\]
\end{itemize}
Combining, $\vf=\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}\,\vu=\mat{K}\vu$.

\paragraph{$\mat{B}\T$ and $\mat{B}$ are transposes, and not by accident.} The
\index{virtual work!discrete}\index{transpose and adjoint}%
internal work done stretching the springs equals the external work done at
the nodes,
\[
  \vect{\sigma}\T\ve=\vect{\sigma}\T\mat{B}\vu=\vf\T\vu ,
\]
and this identity holds for \emph{every} displacement $\vu$ only if the
equilibrium operator is exactly $\mat{B}\T$. This is the discrete principle of
virtual work; its continuum version is the pair
$\nabla\leftrightarrow-\dive$ of Appendix~\ref{app:ibp}.

With unit stiffnesses, $\mat{K}=\mat{B}\T\mat{B}=\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$:
\[
  \mat{K}=\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
For a uniform unit load $\vf=(1,1,1)\T$, symmetry ($u_1=u_3$) and
back-substitution give $\vu=(\tfrac32,\,2,\,\tfrac32)\T$, a discrete
parabola. As the number of masses grows it converges to the solution
$u(x)=\tfrac12x(1-x)$ of $-u''=1$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$; the same matrix reappears as
the finite element stiffness matrix in Section~\ref{sec:var-fem}.

Figure~\ref{fig:tonti-discrete} shows the structure. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:AtCA}, $\mat{K}=\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}$ is symmetric
and positive definite as soon as $\mat{C}>0$ and $\mat{B}$ has independent
columns, i.e.\ as soon as the structure has no rigid-body motion. The
associated energy is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W-discrete}
  W(\vu)=\tfrac12\vu\T\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}\,\vu-\vu\T\vf
        =\tfrac12\ve\T\vect{\sigma}-\vu\T\vf ,
\end{equation}
the internal potential energy minus the work of the external forces.

\subsection{The minimum of the energy}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:min-discrete}
\index{energy!minimum (discrete)}\index{minimum principle!discrete}%
Let $\mat{K}$ be symmetric positive definite and
$W(\vu)=\tfrac12\vu\T\mat{K}\vu-\vu\T\vf$. Then $W$ is minimal exactly at the
solution of $\mat{K}\vu=\vf$, and $W_{\min}=-\tfrac12\vf\T\mat{K}^{-1}\vf$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mat{K}\vu=\vf$ and let $\vv$ be arbitrary. Using $\mat{K}\vu=\vf$ to
cancel the cross terms,
\[
  W(\vv)-W(\vu)=\tfrac12(\vv-\vu)\T\mat{K}(\vv-\vu)\ge0 ,
\]
with equality only for $\vv=\vu$. Substituting $\vu=\mat{K}^{-1}\vf$ gives
$W_{\min}=\tfrac12\vf\T\mat{K}^{-1}\vf-\vf\T\mat{K}^{-1}\vf$.
\end{proof}

The positive definiteness of $\mat{K}$ can be quantified. Expanding $\vu$ in
\index{Rayleigh quotient}\index{coercivity!discrete}\index{boundedness!discrete}%
the orthonormal eigenbasis of $\mat{K}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rayleigh}
  \lambda_{\min}\,|\vu|^2\le\vu\T\mat{K}\vu\le\lambda_{\max}\,|\vu|^2 .
\end{equation}
The lower bound is \emph{coercivity}, the upper bound \emph{boundedness}:
these are exactly the two hypotheses of the Lax--Milgram theorem of
Section~\ref{sec:var-lm}, here verified on a matrix
(Exercise~\ref{exo:lm-constants}).

\subsection{Symmetry, energy and path independence}\label{ssec:symmetry}
\index{symmetry!and existence of an energy}\index{path independence}%
Positive definiteness makes $W$ a genuine energy, bounded below and strictly
convex. Symmetry is what makes it exist at all. For a linear system
$\vF=\mat{K}\vu$, the work along a loading path from state $A$ to state $B$ is
\[
  \int_A^B\vF\cdot\dot\vu\dd t=\int_A^B\dot\vu\T\mat{K}\vu\dd t
  =\tfrac12\bigl[\vu\T\mat{K}\vu\bigr]_A^B
\]
\emph{if and only if $\mat{K}$ is symmetric}. Otherwise the work depends on
the path and not only on the end points, and no state function exists. The
same holds for bilinear forms: a symmetric form $a(u,v)$ has the energy
$J(u)=\tfrac12a(u,u)-L(u)$, a non-symmetric one generally has none
(Exercises~\ref{exo:guess-energy} and~\ref{exo:no-energy}).

Loading a structure at $P$ and measuring the displacement at $Q$ gives the
same cross-work as loading at $Q$ and measuring at $P$
(Figure~\ref{fig:reciprocity}). This is the reciprocity principle. In class
it is shown by a demonstration called \emph{elastic reciprocity for rods}. A
cantilever rod is loaded at $P$ and the deflection is read at $Q$; then the
same load is moved to $Q$ and the deflection is read at $P$. The two readings
coincide. The presentation follows Fung~\cite{Fung1965}. The theorem has a
long history. Clebsch stated it for elastic
bodies in his treatise of 1862~\cite{Clebsch1862}; Maxwell formulated it for
frames, as the equality of the cross-displacements, in 1864~\cite{Maxwell1864};
Betti proved the general reciprocal work theorem of elasticity in
1872~\cite{Betti1872}; and Rayleigh extended it to vibrating systems in
1873~\cite{Rayleigh1873}. Tarnai and Lengyel~\cite{TarnaiLengyel2007} review
the whole family of reciprocal theorems.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reciprocity}
\index{reciprocity!and symmetry}\index{flexibility matrix}%
Let $\vF=\mat{K}\vu$ with $\mat{K}$ invertible. The reciprocity identity
\[
  \vF^{(1)}\cdot\vu^{(2)}=\vF^{(2)}\cdot\vu^{(1)}
\]
holds for every pair of load cases if and only if $\mat{K}\T=\mat{K}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\vF^{(1)}\cdot\vu^{(2)}=(\mat{K}\vu^{(1)})\T\vu^{(2)}=\vu^{(1)\top}\mat{K}\T\vu^{(2)}$
and $\vF^{(2)}\cdot\vu^{(1)}=\vu^{(1)\top}\mat{K}\vu^{(2)}$. They agree for all
$\vu^{(1)},\vu^{(2)}$ if and only if $\mat{K}\T=\mat{K}$. For unit loads at $P$
and $Q$, the displacements are columns of the flexibility matrix
$\mat{S}=\mat{K}^{-1}$, and reciprocity reads $S_{QP}=S_{PQ}$.
\end{proof}
In the continuum, reciprocity is Green's second identity
(Appendix~\ref{app:ibp}). When the body contains an unknown defect,
reciprocity between the true field and a known ``adjoint'' field is
\emph{broken}, and the gap measures the defect: this is the idea of
Chapter~\ref{ch:rg}.

\subsection{Circuits and trusses: the same linear algebra}
\paragraph{A resistor network.} Replace masses by nodes, displacements $\vu$ by
\index{resistor network}%
potentials and springs by resistors. Ohm's law $\sigma_i=c_ie_i$ is the
constitutive law, $\mat{C}=\diag(\text{conductances})$, and Kirchhoff's current
law at the nodes is exactly $\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\vf$ (injected currents).
With unit conductances the assembled matrix is the same $\mat{K}$: a resistor
ladder and a spring chain obey identical linear algebra. It is the discrete
analogue of $-\dive(\tA(\vx)\nabla u)=f$ (Section~\ref{sec:var-elliptic}).

\paragraph{A small truss.} Let $\mat{B}$ map joint displacements to bar
\index{truss}\index{energy!complementary (discrete)}%
elongations in a planar truss, $\mat{C}=\diag(E_iA_i/L_i)$ collect the bar
stiffnesses, and $\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\vf$ express the equilibrium of the
joints. Minimizing $W(\vu)$ is the discrete \emph{potential energy principle}.
Its dual, maximizing $-\tfrac12\vect{\sigma}\T\mat{C}^{-1}\vect{\sigma}$ over bar
tensions satisfying $\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\vf$, is the discrete
\emph{complementary energy principle}: the finite-dimensional counterpart of
the primal/dual pair of Section~\ref{sec:var-dual}, with $\mat{B}\T$ playing
the role of $-\dive$ and $\mat{C}^{-1}$ that of the conjugate energy.

\paragraph{A concrete truss.}
\index{truss!three-bar}\index{statically indeterminate structure}%
Figure~\ref{fig:truss} gives a three-bar truss with one free joint. With
equal bar stiffnesses $E_iA_i/L_i=c$,
\[
  \mat{K}=c\,\mat{B}\T\mat{B}=c\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The vertical load $\vf=(0,-F)$ gives $\vu=\bigl(0,-F/(2c)\bigr)$ and the tensions
$\vect{\sigma}=c\mat{B}\vu=F\,(\tfrac1{2\sqrt2},\tfrac12,\tfrac1{2\sqrt2})$;
one checks $\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\vf$. Equilibrium alone does not fix
$\vect{\sigma}$: the self-stress $\vect{\sigma}_0=(1,-\sqrt2,1)$ satisfies
$\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}_0=\vzero$ and can be added freely. The minimum of the
complementary energy selects the actual tensions among all these
equilibrated ones.

\subsection{Constraints and Lagrange multipliers}\label{sec:var-lagrange}
\index{constraint}\index{Lagrange multiplier}\index{Lagrangian}%
Many problems minimize an energy under constraints: imposed displacements,
incompressibility, contact, plasticity. The Lagrangian turns the constrained
problem into an unconstrained saddle-point problem.

\begin{gbox}{Constrained optimization}
\index{Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions}\index{saddle point}\index{complementarity condition}%
Let $C$ and $\vect{A}=(A_1,\dots,A_m)$ be convex functions on $\R^n$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Problem $\mathcal P$:} minimize $C(\vx)$ under the constraint
$\vect{A}(\vx)\le\vb$.
\item \emph{Lagrangian:}
$\mathcal L(\vx,\vect{y})=C(\vx)+\vect{y}\cdot\bigl(\vect{A}(\vx)-\vb\bigr)$,
$\vect{y}\in\R^m$.
\item \emph{Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions.} If $\vx$ solves $\mathcal P$
(and a qualification condition holds), there is $\vect{y}\ge\vzero$ with
\[
  \nabla C(\vx)+\sum_iy_i\nabla A_i(\vx)=\vzero,\qquad
  \vect{y}\ge\vzero,\quad\vect{A}(\vx)\le\vb,\quad
  y_i\bigl(A_i(\vx)-b_i\bigr)=0\ \ \forall i .
\]
\end{itemize}
For equality constraints $\vect{A}(\vx)=\vb$ the multipliers have no sign,
and the conditions reduce to $\nabla_{\vx}\mathcal L=\vzero$,
$\nabla_{\vect{y}}\mathcal L=\vzero$: $(\vx,\vect{y})$ is a saddle point of
$\mathcal L$.
\end{gbox}
\paragraph{Sign of the multiplier and reaction.}
\index{Lagrange multiplier!sign convention}%
In mechanics $C$ is the potential energy, so $\nabla C(\vx)$ is the internal
force minus the external force, and equilibrium with a reaction $\vect{r}$
reads $\nabla C(\vx)=\vect{r}$. The KKT conditions then give the reaction
$\vect{r}=-\sum_iy_i\nabla A_i(\vx)$. With the standard convention of the box,
$y_i\ge0$ is the magnitude of the reaction, which pushes against the
constraint, opposite to $\nabla A_i$. For equality constraints we write the
Lagrangian with a minus sign,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian-eq}
  \mathcal L(\vx,\vlambda)=C(\vx)-\vlambda\cdot\bigl(\mat{P}\vx-\vb\bigr),
\end{equation}
so that stationarity reads $\nabla C(\vx)=\mat{P}\T\vlambda$: the multiplier is
directly the reaction, $\vect{r}=\mat{P}\T\vlambda$. This convention is used
in the rest of the chapter.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=5cm]{figures/ch1/lagrange.pdf}
\caption{Level sets of a convex cost $C$ and a constraint. At the constrained
minimum the gradient of $C$ is balanced by a multiple of the gradient of the
constraint.}
\label{fig:lagrange}
\end{figure}

The last condition is \emph{complementarity}: either the constraint is
active, $A_i(\vx)=b_i$, or its multiplier vanishes. The multiplier is the
force needed to enforce the constraint. In Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptic},
imposed displacements in a Ritz approximation are enforced this way, and the
multipliers give the reaction forces (Section~\ref{ssec:ritz-lagrange}).
Exercises~\ref{exo:kkt1}--\ref{exo:kkt4} work through four cases.

\paragraph{Example: an imposed displacement in the spring chain.}
\index{Lagrange multiplier!reaction force}\index{Tonti diagram!with constraints}%
Impose the displacement of the third mass, $u_3=\uD$, instead of a force.
The constraint is linear, $\mat{P}\vu=\uD$ with $\mat{P}=(0\ 0\ 1)$, and the
Lagrangian~\eqref{eq:lagrangian-eq} of the energy~\eqref{eq:W-discrete} is
\[
  \mathcal L(\vu,\lambda)=\tfrac12\vu\T\mat{K}\vu-\vf\T\vu-\lambda\,(\mat{P}\vu-\uD).
\]
Its stationarity conditions, $\mat{K}\vu-\vf-\mat{P}\T\lambda=\vzero$ and
$\mat{P}\vu=\uD$, form the symmetric saddle-point system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:saddle-springs}
  \begin{bmatrix}\mat{K}&-\mat{P}\T\\ -\mat{P}&0\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}\vu\\ \lambda\end{bmatrix}=
  \begin{bmatrix}\vf\\ -\uD\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The first line is the equilibrium of the masses,
$\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\vf+\vect{r}$, with the reaction
$\vect{r}=\mat{P}\T\lambda$: the multiplier is the force that the support
must exert to impose the displacement. With unit stiffnesses, $\vf=\vzero$ and
$\uD=1$: $\vu=(\tfrac13,\tfrac23,1)\T$ and $\lambda=+\tfrac43$. The support
pushes the third mass in the direction of $x$ with the force
$r_3=\lambda=\tfrac43$, the stiffness of the three left springs in series
($\tfrac13$) plus that of the right spring ($1$).
The Tonti diagram keeps its structure (Figure~\ref{fig:tonti-constraint}):
the constraint enters the kinematic column, its multiplier the static one.
The same construction imposes displacements in a finite element code
(Section~\ref{ssec:ritz-lagrange}).

\section{Function spaces in a nutshell}\label{sec:var-spaces}
\index{function space}\index{Sobolev space}%
In the spring chain the unknown is a vector of $\R^3$ and the energy is
finite for every $\vu$. In the continuum the unknown is a function, and the
first question is: \emph{for which functions is the energy finite?} The
answer defines the space in which the problem is posed. Three spaces suffice
for this book; their definitions, the proofs and the precise statements are
in Appendix~\ref{app:spaces}.

\paragraph{$L^2(\Omega)$: finite mean square.}
\index{function space!L2@$L^2$}%
$v\in L^2(\Omega)$ if $\displaystyle \norm{v}_{L^2}^2=\int_\Omega v^2\dd x<\infty$. It is the
continuum version of the Euclidean norm $|\vu|^2=\sum u_i^2$. A function may be
discontinuous or even unbounded and still belong to $L^2$:
$x^{-1/4}\in L^2(0,1)$, but $x^{-1/2}\notin L^2(0,1)$ since
$\displaystyle \int_0^1x^{-1}\dd x=\infty$.

\paragraph{$H^1(\Omega)$: finite energy.}
\index{function space!H1@$H^1$}%
$v\in H^1(\Omega)$ if $v$ and its gradient are in $L^2$:
$\displaystyle \norm{v}_{H^1}^2=\int_\Omega\bigl(v^2+|\nabla v|^2\bigr)\dd x<\infty$. This is
exactly the condition for the Dirichlet energy~\eqref{eq:poisson-energy} to be
finite. The gradient is the continuum version of $\mat{B}$, and
$\displaystyle \int|\nabla v|^2$ that of $|\mat{B}\vu|^2$.

\paragraph{$H^1_0(\Omega)$: finite energy and zero boundary values.}
\index{function space!H10@$H^1_0$}\index{trace}%
$H^1_0(\Omega)$ contains the $v\in H^1(\Omega)$ with $v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$:
the essential boundary condition is built into the space, as the fixed walls
are built into the matrix $\mat{B}$ of the spring chain. Boundary values of
$H^1$ functions (traces) are well defined, in a space called
$H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)$.

\paragraph{The Poincar\'e inequality.}
\index{Poincar\'e inequality}%
In $H^1_0(\Omega)$ the slope controls the function:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poincare}
  \norm{v}_{L^2}\le C_P\,\norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}\qquad\forall v\in H^1_0(\Omega),
\end{equation}
with $C_P$ depending only on $\Omega$ ($C_P=L/\pi$ on an interval of length
$L$). In words: a function pinned to zero on the boundary cannot be large
without having slopes. Without the boundary condition the inequality fails:
a constant has zero gradient and a nonzero norm. This is exactly the
discrete statement that $\mat{B}$ has independent columns, i.e.\ that the
structure has no rigid-body motion, and $1/C_P^2$ plays the role of
$\lambda_{\min}(\mat{B}\T\mat{B})$. The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:spaces}.

\section{Weak and strong forms: the right function space}\label{sec:var-weak}
\index{weak form}\index{strong form}\index{Sobolev space}\index{integration by parts!weak form}%
Integration by parts lowers the number of derivatives required, moving the
natural setting from $H^2$ down to $H^1$. This is why Sobolev spaces and
elliptic PDE theory are so intertwined~\cite{AdamsFournier2003,Evans2010}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=1.6cm]
  \node[smallbox] (s) {Strong form \\ $-\Delta u = f,\ u \in H^2(\Omega)$};
  \node[smallbox, right=of s] (w) {Weak form \\
    $\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v = \int_\Omega f v,\ u,v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$};
  \draw[arr] (s) -- node[above, font=\small]{integrate} node[below,font=\small]{by parts} (w);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Multiply $-\Delta u=f$ by a test function $v\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ and
integrate by parts (Green's first identity, Theorem~\ref{thm:green1}):
\[
  -\int_\Omega (\Delta u)\, v\dd x=\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\dd x
  = \int_\Omega f v\dd x .
\]
The boundary term vanishes since $v=0$ near $\partial\Omega$. The left-hand
side only requires $u,v\in H^1$ (one derivative in $L^2$), so the identity
extends by density to all $v\in H^1_0(\Omega)$; there is no longer any need
for $u\in H^2$.

The weak form is also the first variation of the energy~\eqref{eq:poisson-energy}:
$\displaystyle \frac{\dd}{\dd t}J(u+tv)\big|_{t=0}=\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-\int_\Omega fv$.
The three settings
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\sffamily energy & \sffamily weak form (virtual work) & \sffamily strong form (Euler--Lagrange)\\
\midrule
$\displaystyle u=\argmin_{v\in V}J(v)$ & $\delta J(u)[v]=0\ \ \forall v\in V$ & $-\Delta u=f$ in $\Omega$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
are equivalent for smooth solutions; their precise definitions are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:derivatives}.

\section{Existence by minimization: the direct method}\label{sec:var-direct}
\index{direct method}\index{lower semicontinuity}\index{minimizing sequence}\index{Poincar\'e inequality}\index{coercivity!of an energy}%
Recasting a PDE as a minimization problem turns existence into the question
of whether a functional attains its infimum. Weak compactness and lower
semicontinuity answer it without constructing the solution~\cite{Dacorogna2008}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.55cm]
  \node[box] (a) {Minimize $J(u)$ over $V$};
  \node[box, below=of a] (b) {Take a minimizing sequence $(u_n)$};
  \node[box, below=of b] (c) {Bounded $\Rightarrow$ weakly compact in $V$ (reflexive)};
  \node[box, below=of c] (d) {$J$ weakly lower semicontinuous};
  \node[box, goal, below=of d] (e) {Minimizer $u^\ast$ attained};
  \draw[arr] (a) -- (b); \draw[arr] (b) -- (c); \draw[arr] (c) -- (d); \draw[arr] (d) -- (e);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
For the energy~\eqref{eq:poisson-energy} on $V=H^1_0(\Omega)$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{coercivity:} by the Poincar\'e inequality~\eqref{eq:poincare},
  $\norm{v}_{L^2}\le C_P\norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}$ (a function vanishing on the
  boundary is controlled by its slope),
  \[
    J(v)\ge\tfrac12\norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}^2-C_P\norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}\to\infty
    \quad\text{as }\norm{v}_{H^1_0}\to\infty;
  \]
  in particular $J$ is bounded below;
  \item a minimizing sequence $(u_n)$ is therefore bounded in $H^1_0$, and a
  subsequence converges weakly to some $u$ (reflexivity);
  \item $J$ is convex and continuous, hence weakly lower semicontinuous:
  $J(u)\le\liminf J(u_n)=\inf J$.
\end{itemize}
So $u$ is a minimizer, and its first variation gives $-\Delta u=f$ in the weak
sense.

\paragraph{In matrix terms.} For $W(\vu)=\tfrac12\vu\T\mat{K}\vu-\vf\T\vu$ in
$\R^n$ the same three steps are elementary: $W\ge\tfrac12\lambda_{\min}|\vu|^2-|\vf||\vu|$
grows at infinity (coercivity), so a minimizing sequence stays in a ball;
a ball of $\R^n$ is compact, so a subsequence converges; and $W$ is
continuous, so the limit is a minimizer. In infinite dimension a bounded
sequence need not converge, and \emph{weak} convergence with \emph{lower
semicontinuity} replaces compactness and continuity.

\paragraph{When lower semicontinuity fails: the Bolza problem.}
\index{Bolza problem}\index{minimizing sequence!non-convergent}\index{lower semicontinuity!failure}\index{convexity!and lower semicontinuity}%
Convexity of the energy density in the gradient is what makes $J$ weakly
lower semicontinuous. Without it the direct method can fail. Take
\[
  J(u)=\int_0^1\Bigl(\bigl((u')^2-1\bigr)^2+u^2\Bigr)\dd x,
  \qquad u(0)=u(1)=0 .
\]
The density $\bigl((u')^2-1\bigr)^2$ is a double well in the slope, with
minima at $u'=\pm1$. The sawtooth $u_n$ with $n$ teeth of slopes $\pm1$
(Figure~\ref{fig:bolza}) has $\bigl((u_n')^2-1\bigr)^2=0$ almost everywhere
and height $1/(2n)$, so
\[
  J(u_n)=\int_0^1u_n^2\dd x=\frac{1}{12n^2}\to0=\inf J .
\]
The sequence is bounded in $H^1$ and $u_n\to0$ uniformly; $u_n'$ oscillates
between $\pm1$ and converges only weakly, to $0$. But $J(0)=1$: the infimum is
not attained. No function has $J=0$, since this needs $|u'|=1$ and $u=0$ at
the same time. The minimizing sequence develops finer and finer oscillations
instead of converging: this is the mechanism of fine microstructures, such
as twinned martensite. The direct method works for the energies of this
chapter because they are convex in the gradient.
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch1/bolza.pdf}
\caption{A minimizing sequence that does not converge to a minimizer. (a)
Sawtooth functions $u_n$ with slopes $\pm1$; they tend uniformly to $0$. (b)
The energy density is a double well in the slope: $u_n'$ stays in the wells,
its weak limit $u'=0$ sits on the hump. (c) $J(u_n)=1/(12n^2)\to0$, while the

\section{The Lax--Milgram theorem}\label{sec:var-lm}
\index{Lax--Milgram theorem}\index{coercivity!of a bilinear form}\index{boundedness!of a bilinear form}%
Two functional-analytic hypotheses on a bilinear form, boundedness and
coercivity, give existence \emph{and} uniqueness, independently of the
specific PDE~\cite{LaxMilgram1954}.

\begin{gbox}{Lax--Milgram theorem}
Let $V$ be a Hilbert space, $a:V\times V\to\R$ bilinear and $L\in V^\ast$.
If $a$ is \emph{bounded}, $|a(u,v)|\le M\norm{u}\norm{v}$, and \emph{coercive},
$a(u,u)\ge\alpha\norm{u}^2$ with $\alpha>0$, then there is a unique $u\in V$
such that
\[
  a(u,v)=L(v)\qquad\forall v\in V,
\]
and $\norm{u}\le\norm{L}_{V^\ast}/\alpha$. The form $a$ need not be symmetric.
\end{gbox}

For the model problem, $\displaystyle a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v$ and
$\displaystyle L(v)=\int_\Omega fv$ on $H^1_0(\Omega)$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{bounded:} $|a(u,v)|\le\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}\norm{\nabla v}_{L^2}$
  (Cauchy--Schwarz);
  \item \emph{coercive:} $a(u,u)=\norm{\nabla u}_{L^2}^2\ge\alpha\norm{u}_{H^1_0}^2$
  (Poincar\'e);
  \item $L$ is bounded: $|L(v)|\le\norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{L^2}\le C_P\norm{f}_{L^2}\norm{v}_{H^1_0}$.
\end{itemize}
Lax--Milgram gives existence and uniqueness directly, without mentioning
minimization.

\paragraph{In matrix terms.} For $a(\vu,\vv)=\vv\T\mat{K}\vu$ on $\R^n$,
boundedness is $\lambda_{\max}<\infty$ (always true in finite dimension) and
coercivity is $\vu\T\mat{K}\vu\ge\alpha|\vu|^2$, i.e.\ the symmetric part of
$\mat{K}$ is positive definite. Then $\mat{K}\vu=\vf$ has a unique solution
with $|\vu|\le|\vf|/\alpha$: this is the whole content of Lax--Milgram in
$\R^n$. The bounds~\eqref{eq:rayleigh} are the two hypotheses, and the theorem
is needed only because the constants must not degenerate as the dimension
goes to infinity (as the mesh is refined).

\subsection*{What goes wrong without coercivity}
Boundedness alone is not enough. Two examples, one linear and one nonlinear,
show what breaks.

\paragraph{(a) A linear resonance.} On $H^1_0(0,1)$ take
\index{resonance}\index{Wirtinger inequality}\index{Fredholm alternative}%
\[
  a(u,v)=\int_0^1u'v'\dd x-\pi^2\int_0^1uv\dd x,
\]
corresponding to $-u''-\pi^2u=f$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$. The form is bounded,
$|a(u,v)|\le(1+\pi^2)\norm{u}_{H^1_0}\norm{v}_{H^1_0}$, but not coercive: the
sharp Poincar\'e (Wirtinger) inequality $\displaystyle \int_0^1(u')^2\ge\pi^2\int_0^1u^2$ is
an equality at $u=\sin\pi x$, so $a(\sin\pi x,\sin\pi x)=0$. This is not an
artefact of the proof: $\pi^2$ is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
$-\dd^2/\dd x^2$ on $(0,1)$, the problem is at resonance, and the Fredholm
alternative (Section~\ref{sec:var-elliptic}) takes over. Solvability requires
$f\perp\sin\pi x$ in $L^2$. For $f=1$, $\displaystyle \int_0^1\sin\pi x\dd x=2/\pi\neq0$:
\textbf{no solution}. For $f=\sin2\pi x$ the condition holds, and
$u_c=\sin(2\pi x)/(3\pi^2)+c\sin\pi x$ solves the problem for every
$c\in\R$: \textbf{infinitely many solutions} (Figure~\ref{fig:resonance}).

\paragraph{Physical examples.}
\index{resonance!vibrating string}\index{buckling!Euler load}%
Two classical situations lead exactly to this equation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{A string driven at its fundamental frequency.} A string of
tension $T$, linear density $\rho$ and length $L$, fixed at both ends and
loaded by $p(x)\cos\omega t$, vibrates as $w(x)\cos\omega t$ with
$-Tw''-\rho\omega^2w=p$. On the unit interval, $\rho\omega^2/T=\pi^2$ means
$\displaystyle \omega=\omega_1=\frac{\pi}{L}\sqrt{T/\rho}$, the fundamental frequency. Without
damping, a load that excites the first mode ($\displaystyle \int p\sin\pi x\neq0$) has no
periodic response: the amplitude grows without bound. A load orthogonal to
the mode produces a response defined up to a free oscillation in the mode.
\item \emph{A column at the Euler load.} A pinned column of length $L$ and
bending stiffness $EI$, compressed by $P$ and bent by the moment $M(x)$ of
lateral loads, deflects as $-EIw''-Pw=M$. On the unit interval the
operator is singular for $P/EI=\pi^2$, i.e.\ at the Euler load
$P_E=\pi^2EI/L^2$~\cite{TimoshenkoGere1961}. Below $P_E$ the lateral deflection
is amplified by the factor $1/(1-P/P_E)$; at $P_E$ a symmetric lateral load
has no equilibrium, and without lateral load every $w=c\sin(\pi x/L)$ is in
equilibrium: neutral equilibrium, the onset of buckling.
\end{itemize}
In both cases the loss of coercivity is a physical event: resonance or
buckling. Stability and buckling are treated in the last part of the
course.

\paragraph{(b) A nonlinear double well.} Lax--Milgram is a linear theorem, but
\index{double well}\index{Allen--Cahn equation}\index{second variation}\index{stability!of a critical point}%
the coercivity intuition reappears in the second variation. On $(0,1)$ take
\[
  E[u]=\int_0^1\Bigl(\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}(u')^2+W(u)\Bigr)\dd x,
  \qquad W(u)=\tfrac14(u^2-1)^2,
\]
whose Euler--Lagrange equation is the stationary Allen--Cahn equation
$-\varepsilon^2u''+u^3-u=0$~\cite{AllenCahn1979}. Existence of a minimizer
still holds by the direct method (Rellich--Kondrachov compactness handles the
non-convex zeroth-order term), but uniqueness fails twice: by the symmetry
$u\mapsto-u$, $u\equiv1$ and $u\equiv-1$ are both global minimizers; and
$u\equiv0$ is a critical point whose second variation
\[
  \delta^2E(0)[v,v]=\varepsilon^2\int_0^1(v')^2\dd x-\int_0^1v^2\dd x
\]
is \emph{negative} for every nonzero constant $v$: $u\equiv0$ is unstable
(Figure~\ref{fig:doublewell}). Stability through the second variation is the
subject of the last part of the course.

\paragraph{Physical examples.}
\index{double well!post-buckling}\index{snap-through}\index{phase transition}%
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The buckled column.} Beyond the Euler load, write the deflection
of the pinned column as $w=a\sin(\pi x/L)$. The bending energy
$\displaystyle \tfrac12EI\int(w'')^2$ minus the work of $P$ on the shortening
$\displaystyle \tfrac12\int(w')^2$ gives, to leading orders,
\[
  \Pi(a)=\frac{\pi^2}{4L}\,(P_E-P)\,a^2+\beta\,a^4,\qquad\beta>0,
\]
where the quartic term comes from the geometric nonlinearity of the elastica.
For $P<P_E$ the straight column is the unique minimum; for $P>P_E$, $\Pi$ is a
double well: the straight position $a=0$ is unstable, and the column buckles
to the left or to the right, $a=\pm a^\ast$ with $a^{\ast2}\propto P-P_E$. The
resonance example above is the linearization of this problem at $P=P_E$.
\item \emph{Bistable structures.} A shallow arch or a two-bar (von Mises)
truss under a vertical load has two stable equilibria and snaps through from
one to the other: the energy along the snapping path is a double well.
\item \emph{Phase transitions.} In a binary alloy, $u$ is the local
concentration and the two wells are the two phases; the gradient term
$\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2}(u')^2$ is the energy of the interface between them.
This is the original setting of the Allen--Cahn
equation~\cite{AllenCahn1979}; twinned martensite in shape-memory alloys is
its elastic analogue.
\end{itemize}

\section{General elliptic operators}\label{sec:var-elliptic}
\index{elliptic operator!general}\index{uniform ellipticity}\index{G\aa rding inequality@G{\aa}rding inequality}%
The Poisson problem uses the simplest elliptic operator. The Lax--Milgram
framework is built for the general second-order case, where the minimization
approach does not always apply~\cite{GilbargTrudinger2001,Evans2010}.

\begin{gbox}{The elliptic operator and its bilinear form}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:general-elliptic}
  -\dive(\tA(\vx)\nabla u)+\vb(\vx)\cdot\nabla u+c(\vx)u=f
  \ \text{in } \Omega,\qquad u=0 \ \text{on } \partial\Omega,
\end{equation}
with $\tA(\vx)$ a second-order tensor field (conductivity, diffusivity),
$\vb$ a drift (convection) field and $c$ a reaction coefficient. The operator
is \emph{uniformly elliptic} if
$\vxi\cdot\tA(\vx)\vxi\ge\alpha|\vxi|^2$ for all $\vxi\in\R^n$, $\vx\in\Omega$,
some $\alpha>0$. Integrating by parts with Theorem~\ref{thm:ibp-matrix} gives
the bilinear form on $H^1_0(\Omega)$
\[
  a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\tA\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\dd x
  +\int_\Omega(\vb\cdot\nabla u)\,v\dd x+\int_\Omega c\,uv\dd x .
\]
\end{gbox}

\paragraph{The hypotheses.} If $\tA,\vb,c\in L^\infty(\Omega)$, $a$ is bounded.
Ellipticity gives $\displaystyle \int\tA\nabla u\cdot\nabla u\ge\alpha\norm{\nabla u}^2_{L^2}$,
and Poincar\'e controls the full norm; but the lower-order terms can spoil
coercivity. G\aa rding's inequality~\cite{Garding1953},
\[
  a(u,u)\ge\alpha\norm{u}_{H^1_0}^2-\lambda\norm{u}_{L^2}^2,\qquad\lambda\ge0,
\]
states coercivity up to a compact perturbation. Either one shifts the form,
$\displaystyle a_\lambda(u,v)=a(u,v)+\lambda\int uv$, which is coercive, or one invokes the
\emph{Fredholm alternative}~\cite{Evans2010,Brezis2011}.

\begin{gbox}{Fredholm alternative}
\index{Fredholm alternative}\index{eigenvalue problem!Fredholm alternative}\index{adjoint operator!Fredholm alternative}%
Let $a$ satisfy G\aa rding's inequality on $H^1_0(\Omega)$, $\Omega$ bounded.
For $\mu\in\R$ consider the problem: find $u\in H^1_0(\Omega)$ with
\[
  a(u,v)-\mu\int_\Omega uv\dd x=\int_\Omega fv\dd x\qquad\forall v\in H^1_0(\Omega).
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item The values of $\mu$ for which the homogeneous problem ($f=0$) has a
nonzero solution form a \emph{discrete} set: the eigenvalues
$\mu_1,\mu_2,\dots$, with no finite accumulation point.
\item For $\mu$ \emph{outside} this set, the problem has a unique solution for
every $f\in L^2(\Omega)$, and $\norm{u}_{H^1}\le C(\mu)\norm{f}_{L^2}$.
\item For $\mu=\mu_k$ an eigenvalue, the kernel $N_k$ of the problem and the
kernel $N_k^\ast$ of the adjoint problem (with $a(v,w)$ in place of
$a(w,v)$) have the same finite dimension. The problem is solvable \emph{if
and only if} $f$ is orthogonal in $L^2$ to $N_k^\ast$, and the solution is
then defined up to an element of $N_k$.
\end{itemize}
For a symmetric form, $N_k^\ast=N_k$ is the eigenspace of $\mu_k$.
\end{gbox}
The resonance example of Section~\ref{sec:var-lm} is the case $\displaystyle a(u,v)=\int_0^1u'v'$,
$\mu=\pi^2=\mu_1$, $N_1=N_1^\ast=\operatorname{span}\{\sin\pi x\}$: solvable iff
$f\perp\sin\pi x$, and then up to $c\sin\pi x$.

\paragraph{Where Lax--Milgram goes further than minimization.} Lax--Milgram
\index{convection--diffusion}\index{symmetry!of a bilinear form}%
does not require $a$ to be symmetric; minimization does. A quadratic energy
$J(u)=\tfrac12a(u,u)-L(u)$ has $\delta J(u)[v]=a(u,v)-L(v)$ only when $a$ is
symmetric (Section~\ref{ssec:symmetry}). The convection term
$\displaystyle \int(\vb\cdot\nabla u)v$ is not symmetric, so convection--diffusion has no
natural energy, yet Lax--Milgram applies. Dirichlet, Neumann or Robin
conditions are handled by changing the space ($H^1_0$ or $H^1$) and adding
boundary terms to $a$ (Exercises~\ref{exo:neumann} and~\ref{exo:robin}).

\section{Duality}\label{sec:var-dual}
\index{duality}\index{energy!complementary}%
Variational problems are optimization problems, so convex duality
applies~\cite{EkelandTemam1999,Rockafellar1970}: the primal (energy) problem
has a dual (complementary energy) problem, and for convex functionals the two
meet at the optimum.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=1.6cm]
  \node[smallbox] (p) {Primal problem \\ $\min_u J(u)$};
  \node[smallbox, right=of p] (d) {Dual problem \\ $\max_{\vp}\,-J^\ast(\vp)$};
  \draw[arr,{Latex[length=2.3mm]}-{Latex[length=2.3mm]}] (p) -- node[above, font=\small]{duality} (d);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\subsection{The Legendre--Fenchel transform}
\begin{gbox}{Legendre--Fenchel transform}
\index{Legendre--Fenchel transform}\index{convex conjugate}\index{Fenchel--Young inequality}%
For a convex function $F$ on a space $X$, its conjugate on the dual space is
\[
  F^\ast(\vp)=\sup_{\vx}\bigl[\vp\cdot\vx-F(\vx)\bigr].
\]
\begin{itemize}
  \item $F^\ast$ is convex (a supremum of affine functions);
  \item Fenchel--Young inequality: $F(\vx)+F^\ast(\vp)\ge\vp\cdot\vx$ for all
  $\vx,\vp$, with equality iff $\vp\in\partial F(\vx)$;
  \item if $F$ is convex and lower semicontinuous, $F^{\ast\ast}=F$:
  $F(\vx)=\sup_{\vp}\bigl[\vp\cdot\vx-F^\ast(\vp)\bigr]$.
\end{itemize}
\end{gbox}
Geometrically, the tangent to $F$ with slope $p$ has vertical intercept
$-F^\ast(p)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:legendre}). Examples are worked out in
Exercise~\ref{exo:lf}.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Constructing the dual of the Poisson problem}
\index{duality!Fenchel--Rockafellar}\index{adjoint operator!of the gradient}%
Split $J$ into two convex pieces joined by the linear operator
$\Lambda=\nabla$:
\[
  J(u)=F(\Lambda u)+G(u),\qquad
  F(\vp)=\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\vp|^2\dd x,\qquad
  G(u)=-\int_\Omega fu\dd x .
\]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\emph{Step \arabic*.},leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{Conjugate of $F$.} A quadratic form on a Hilbert space is
self-conjugate: $\displaystyle F^\ast(\vp)=\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\vp|^2\dd x$.
\item \emph{Conjugate of $G$.} $G$ is linear, so its conjugate is an
indicator function:
$\displaystyle G^\ast(w)=\sup_u\int_\Omega(w+f)u\dd x$ equals $0$ if $w=-f$ and $+\infty$
otherwise.
\item \emph{The adjoint $\Lambda^\ast$.} Integrating by parts (the boundary
term vanishes in $H^1_0$), $\displaystyle \int_\Omega\vp\cdot\nabla u=-\int_\Omega(\dive\vp)\,u$,
so $\Lambda^\ast\vp=-\dive\vp$.
\item \emph{Assemble.} Fenchel--Rockafellar duality states
\[
  \inf_u\bigl[F(\Lambda u)+G(u)\bigr]
  =\sup_{\vp}\bigl[-F^\ast(\vp)-G^\ast(-\Lambda^\ast\vp)\bigr].
\]
The term $G^\ast(-\Lambda^\ast\vp)$ is $0$ exactly when $\dive\vp+f=0$ and
$+\infty$ otherwise: it becomes a constraint.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{gbox}{Primal and dual problems}
\index{primal problem}\index{dual problem}%
\[
  \min_{u\in H^1_0(\Omega)}\Bigl(\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2-\int_\Omega fu\Bigr)
  \;=\;
  \max_{\vp:\ \dive\vp+f=0}\Bigl(-\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\vp|^2\Bigr).
\]
At the optimum $\vp=\nabla u$, and both values equal $\displaystyle -\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2$.
\end{gbox}
There is no duality gap because $F$ is continuous and finite everywhere and
$\Lambda$ is bounded, the standard qualification condition. This is the
statement that the minimum of the potential energy (over kinematically
admissible fields) and the maximum of the complementary energy (over
statically admissible fields) meet at the solution. It is used in elasticity
(Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptic}) and in a posteriori error estimation. Its
discrete counterpart is the truss of Section~\ref{sec:var-discrete}, and
Exercise~\ref{exo:primal-dual} checks it numerically.

\section{Galerkin approximation and finite elements}\label{sec:var-fem}
\index{Galerkin method}\index{C\'ea's lemma@C\'ea's lemma}\index{stiffness matrix}%
The weak formulation is exactly what finite element methods discretize:
restrict the problem to a finite-dimensional subspace, solve a linear system,
and control the error with C\'ea's lemma~\cite{Cea1964}
(see also~\cite{Ciarlet2002,BrennerScott2008}). The subspace can be built
from finite elements, or from the splines of computer-aided design: this is
isogeometric analysis, the subject of the second half of the section.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.55cm]
  \node[box] (a) {Continuous problem in $V$: \ $a(u,v) = L(v)$, infinite-dimensional};
  \node[box, below=of a] (b) {Discrete problem in $V_h \subset V$: \ $a(u_h,v_h) = L(v_h)$};
  \node[box, key, below=of b] (c) {C\'ea's lemma: \ $\norm{u-u_h} \le \frac{M}{\alpha} \inf_{v_h\in V_h} \norm{u-v_h}$};
  \draw[arr] (a) -- (b); \draw[arr] (b) -- (c);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\subsection{Linear finite elements in one dimension}\label{sec:var-fem-1d}
\index{finite element method!one-dimensional}\index{hat function}%
Take the one-dimensional problem $-u''=f$ on $(0,1)$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$, with weak
form $\displaystyle \int_0^1u'v'=\int_0^1fv$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Partition $[0,1]$ into $n$ intervals of size $h=1/n$ and let
  $V_h\subset H^1_0$ be spanned by the piecewise-linear ``hat'' functions
  $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_{n-1}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hat-assembly}, left).
  \item Write $u_h=\sum_jc_j\varphi_j$ and require
  $\displaystyle \int u_h'\varphi_i'=\int f\varphi_i$ for each $i$: this is the linear
  system $\mat{K}\mat{c}=\mat{F}$ with the tridiagonal stiffness matrix
  $\displaystyle K_{ij}=\int\varphi_i'\varphi_j'=h^{-1}\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$,
  the matrix of the spring chain.
  \item C\'ea's lemma gives $\norm{u-u_h}_{H^1_0}\le C\inf_{v_h}\norm{u-v_h}_{H^1_0}=O(h)$
  for smooth $u$; the $L^2$ error and the nodal values converge faster
  (Exercise~\ref{exo:conv}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Assembly, element by element.}
\index{finite element method!assembly}\index{element matrix}%
The matrix is not computed entry by entry but element by element. On the
element $[x_{e-1},x_e]$ only $\varphi_{e-1}$ and $\varphi_e$ are nonzero, with
slopes $-1/h$ and $1/h$. The element contributes the $2\times2$ element matrix
\[
  \mat{k}^e=\int_{x_{e-1}}^{x_e}
  \begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{e-1}'\\ \varphi_e'\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{e-1}' & \varphi_e'\end{bmatrix}\dd x
  =\frac1h\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}
\]
to the rows and columns of its two nodes. Each interior node receives $1/h$
from each of its two elements, hence the $2/h$ on the diagonal. The rows and
columns of the end nodes are then deleted, because $u(0)=u(1)=0$ is built
into $V_h$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hat-assembly}, right). Each element is a spring of
stiffness $1/h$ between two nodes, and the end nodes are the fixed walls:
assembling finite elements is building the spring chain of
Section~\ref{ssec:springs}.
\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch1/hat_assembly.pdf}
\caption{Linear finite elements on $(0,1)$ with four elements. Left: the hat
functions; $\varphi_2$ is shaded, its slope is $\pm1/h$. Right: each
element adds its matrix $\mat{k}^e$ (one colour per element) to the rows and
columns of its two nodes. Deleting the rows and columns of the fixed end
nodes (gray) leaves $h\mat{K}=\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$.}
\label{fig:hat-assembly}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Galerkin orthogonality and C\'ea's lemma}\label{sec:var-cea}
\index{Galerkin method!orthogonality}\index{energy norm}\index{C\'ea's lemma@C\'ea's lemma!as a projection}%
Take $v=v_h\in V_h\subset V$ in the continuous problem and subtract the discrete
one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:galerkin-orth}
  a(u-u_h,v_h)=0\qquad\forall v_h\in V_h .
\end{equation}
This is \emph{Galerkin orthogonality}: the error is orthogonal to the
discrete space. For a symmetric coercive form, $a$ is an inner product, the
\emph{energy inner product}, with the energy norm $\norm{v}_a^2=a(v,v)$. Then
$u_h$ is the orthogonal projection of $u$ onto $V_h$ for this inner product
(Figure~\ref{fig:galerkin-proj}), and Pythagoras gives, for every $v_h\in V_h$,
\[
  \norm{u-v_h}_a^2=\norm{u-u_h}_a^2+\norm{u_h-v_h}_a^2\ \ge\ \norm{u-u_h}_a^2 .
\]

\paragraph{C\'ea's lemma in words.} The Galerkin solution is, up to the factor
$M/\alpha$, the \emph{best} approximation of $u$ that the chosen space can
offer: the error of the method is an approximation error, not an error of
the equations. For a symmetric form the factor disappears in the energy
norm, where $u_h$ is exactly the best approximation (the Ritz method
minimizes the energy). The error is then estimated by interpolating $u$ with
the hat functions, which gives $O(h)$. The proof takes three lines and is
given in Appendix~\ref{app:spaces}.

\paragraph{Other norms.}
\index{Aubin--Nitsche lemma}\index{convergence rate}%
C\'ea's lemma bounds the error in the energy norm only. In $L^2$ the error is
one order better, $\norm{u-u_h}_{L^2}=O(h^2)$ for linear elements: this is
the Aubin--Nitsche duality argument~\cite{Aubin1967,Nitsche1968}
(see~\cite{Ciarlet2002,BrennerScott2008}).
In one dimension with the exact load $\displaystyle \int f\varphi_i$, linear
elements are even exact at the nodes (Exercise~\ref{exo:sp-nodal}).
Finite elements for elasticity, with the matrices $\mat{B}$, $\mat{C}$ and
$\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}$ of Section~\ref{sec:var-discrete}, are presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptic}.

\subsection{What is a finite element?}\label{sec:var-ciarlet}
\index{finite element method!Ciarlet's definition}\index{degrees of freedom}%
The hat functions are one instance of a general construction. Ciarlet's
definition~\cite{Ciarlet2002} makes precise what a finite element is.

\begin{gbox}{Finite element (Ciarlet)}
A \emph{finite element} is a triple $(K,P,\Sigma)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $K\subset\R^n$ is the element domain (an interval, a triangle, a
tetrahedron, a quadrilateral);
\item $P$ is a finite-dimensional space of functions on $K$, usually
polynomials;
\item $\Sigma=\{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N\}$ is a set of linear forms on $P$, the
local \emph{degrees of freedom}, which determine each function of $P$
uniquely: $p\mapsto\bigl(\sigma_1(p),\dots,\sigma_N(p)\bigr)$ is a bijection
from $P$ onto $\R^N$.
\end{itemize}
The local basis is the \emph{nodal basis} $p_1,\dots,p_N$ with
$\sigma_j(p_i)=\delta_{ij}$. The global space is built by gluing elements
through shared degrees of freedom.
\end{gbox}
Linear elements in 1D are $K=[x_{e-1},x_e]$, $P=\mathbb P_1$ and $\Sigma$ the
two end values. Hermite cubics (beams) are $P=\mathbb P_3$ and $\Sigma$ the end
values and end slopes. Two features of this construction matter below. First,
the degrees of freedom are \emph{values} (of the function or of its
derivatives) at points of the element. Second, they are \emph{local}: each
one belongs to an element, or is shared with its neighbours through a node,
and each basis function lives on the elements around its node. Gluing by
nodal values gives $C^0$ continuity across elements. Higher continuity
requires derivative degrees of freedom, which is cumbersome beyond one
dimension.

\subsection{B-splines}\label{sec:var-bsplines}
\index{B-spline}\index{B-spline!Cox--de Boor recursion}\index{knot vector}%
Splines are piecewise polynomials glued with the highest possible smoothness.
They are the basis of computer-aided design (CAD). Their standard basis is
made of B-splines~\cite{deBoor1978,PieglTiller1997}.

\begin{gbox}{B-splines: the Cox--de Boor recursion}
A \emph{knot vector} is a nondecreasing sequence
$\Xi=\{\xi_1\le\xi_2\le\dots\le\xi_{n+p+1}\}$ of parameter values. The $n$
B-spline basis functions of degree $p$ are defined by recursion on the degree:
\begin{align*}
  N_{i,0}(\xi)&=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\xi_i\le\xi<\xi_{i+1},\\
  0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\\
  N_{i,p}(\xi)&=\frac{\xi-\xi_i}{\xi_{i+p}-\xi_i}\,N_{i,p-1}(\xi)
  +\frac{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi}{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi_{i+1}}\,N_{i+1,p-1}(\xi),
\end{align*}
with the convention $0/0=0$ for repeated knots. The knot vector is
\emph{open} if its first and last knots are repeated $p+1$ times.
\end{gbox}

\paragraph{Properties.}
\index{B-spline!continuity}\index{partition of unity}%
\begin{itemize}
\item Each $N_{i,p}$ is a polynomial of degree $p$ on each \emph{knot span}
$[\xi_j,\xi_{j+1})$, nonnegative, and the functions sum to one
(\emph{partition of unity}).
\item \emph{Support:} $N_{i,p}$ vanishes outside $[\xi_i,\xi_{i+p+1}]$, that
is, outside $p+1$ consecutive knot spans.
\item \emph{Continuity:} across a knot of multiplicity $k$, the B-splines are
$C^{p-k}$. With simple knots they are $C^{p-1}$; a knot repeated $p$ times
gives $C^0$, a knot repeated $p+1$ times a discontinuity.
\item \emph{Open knot vectors:} the first and last functions are
interpolatory at the ends, $N_{1,p}(\xi_1)=N_{n,p}(\xi_{n+p+1})=1$, and all
others vanish there.
\item For $p=1$ and simple interior knots, the B-splines are exactly the hat
functions of linear finite elements.
\end{itemize}
Figure~\ref{fig:bsplines} shows the bases for $p=1,2,3$ on an open uniform
knot vector with five knot spans.

\paragraph{Curves and control points.}
\index{control point}%
A B-spline curve is a combination of the basis functions with vector
coefficients, the \emph{control points} $\vect{P}_i$:
\[
  \vect{C}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^nN_{i,p}(\xi)\,\vect{P}_i .
\]
The control points generally do not lie on the curve
(Figure~\ref{fig:bsplines}, panel~(d)). The basis is \emph{not interpolatory}:
the coefficients are not values at points. The curve passes through the
first and last control points only, for an open knot vector.

\paragraph{Why B-splines are not finite elements in Ciarlet's sense.}
\index{B-spline!and Ciarlet's definition}%
B-splines break the structure $(K,P,\Sigma)$ in two ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Their coefficients are not nodal values.} The control points,
and the solution coefficients built in the same way, are \emph{control
variables}, not values at points.
\item \emph{Their basis functions are not element-local.} Each $N_{i,p}$
straddles $p+1$ knot spans, the natural ``elements''. No set of degrees of
freedom belongs to a single element.
\end{itemize}
This overlap is precisely what gives the $C^{p-k}$ continuity across the
knots, instead of the $C^0$ continuity of Lagrange elements.

\subsection{Isogeometric analysis}\label{sec:var-iga}
\index{isogeometric analysis}\index{NURBS}%
Isogeometric analysis (IGA) was introduced by Hughes, Cottrell and
Bazilevs in 2005~\cite{HughesIGA2005,CottrellIGA2009}. It keeps the Galerkin
framework of finite elements and changes the basis: the solution is
expanded on the splines that describe the geometry in CAD.

\paragraph{Geometry comes directly from CAD.}
The domain is described by a patch of non-uniform rational B-splines (NURBS),
the standard representation of CAD.
\begin{gbox}{NURBS geometry}
A two-dimensional NURBS patch maps the parameter square to the domain,
\[
  \vx(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i,j}R_{ij}(\xi,\eta)\,\vect{P}_{ij},
  \qquad
  R_{ij}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{N_i(\xi)\,M_j(\eta)\,w_{ij}}
  {\sum_{k,l}N_k(\xi)\,M_l(\eta)\,w_{kl}},
\]
where $N_i$ and $M_j$ are B-splines in each direction, $\vect{P}_{ij}$ the
control points and $w_{ij}>0$ the \emph{weights}. With all weights equal,
$R_{ij}=N_iM_j$ are tensor-product B-splines. The rational form represents
conics (circles, cylinders, spheres) exactly.
\end{gbox}

\paragraph{The isoparametric idea, reversed.}
\index{isogeometric analysis!isoparametric concept}%
In finite elements the basis chosen for the solution is also used to
approximate the geometry (isoparametric elements). In IGA the basis that
already describes the exact geometry is used for the solution,
\[
  u_h(\vx)=\sum_AR_A(\vxi)\,u_A,\qquad \vx=\vx(\vxi),
\]
where $A$ runs over the functions of the patch, $\vxi=(\xi,\eta)$, and the
$u_A$ are control variables. The geometry is exact at every level of
refinement.

\paragraph{Elements are knot spans.}
The mesh is the set of nonempty knot spans, and their tensor products in 2D
and 3D. These elements are used only for numerical integration, not to
define the basis.

\paragraph{Weak form and assembly are the usual Galerkin ones.}
\index{isogeometric analysis!quadrature}\index{Gauss quadrature}%
Stiffness and load are integrated element by element. Gauss quadrature uses a
\emph{double mapping}: the parent element $[-1,1]^d$ is mapped affinely to
the knot span in the parameter space, which is mapped to the physical
element by $\vx(\vxi)$. For an integrand $g$,
\[
  \int_{\Omega^e}g\dd x=\int_{[-1,1]^d}g\bigl(\vx(\vxi(\tilde\vxi))\bigr)
  \,\Bigl|\det\frac{\partial\vx}{\partial\vxi}\Bigr|\,
  \Bigl|\det\frac{\partial\vxi}{\partial\tilde\vxi}\Bigr|\dd\tilde\xi
  \approx\sum_qw_q\,g_q\,J_q .
\]
A connectivity array lists the $(p+1)^d$ basis functions active on each
element, and the element matrices are assembled as in
Figure~\ref{fig:hat-assembly}.

\paragraph{B\'ezier extraction makes IGA look like finite elements.}
\index{Bezier extraction@B\'ezier extraction}\index{Bernstein polynomials}%
The B-splines are not element-local, but their restrictions to one element
are.
\begin{gbox}{B\'ezier extraction}
On each element, the $(p+1)^d$ active B-splines are linear combinations of the
Bernstein polynomials of degree $p$ on the parent element, which are the same
on every element~\cite{BordenExtraction2011}:
\[
  \mat{N}^e(\tilde\vxi)=\mat{C}^e\,\mat{b}(\tilde\vxi),
\]
with $\mat{N}^e$ the column of active B-splines, $\mat{b}$ the column of
Bernstein polynomials (in 1D, $b_j(t)=\binom pjt^j(1-t)^{p-j}$, $t\in[0,1]$),
and $\mat{C}^e$ the \emph{element extraction operator}, a constant matrix.
\end{gbox}
The code then has an element-local data structure: the Bernstein basis plays
the role of the shape functions, and $\mat{C}^e$ carries the inter-element
smoothness. IGA can thus be plugged into an existing finite element code;
it is the sense in which splines become ``generalized'' finite elements. For
the quadratic B-splines of Exercise~\ref{exo:bsplines}, on the middle element,
$\mat{C}^e=\begin{psmallmatrix}1/2&0&0\\1/2&1&1/2\\0&0&1/2\end{psmallmatrix}$.

\paragraph{Dirichlet conditions need care.}
\index{boundary condition!essential}\index{Nitsche's method}%
Since the basis is not interpolatory, a control variable is not a value of
$u_h$. Homogeneous conditions are easy: with open knot vectors only the
functions of the boundary row are nonzero on the edge of the patch, and
setting their control variables to zero gives $u_h=0$ there. General data
$\uD$ are imposed by one of three approaches:
\begin{itemize}
\item a least-squares or $L^2$ projection of $\uD$ onto the boundary control
variables;
\item weak imposition, for instance by Nitsche's method~\cite{Nitsche1971};
\item Lagrange multipliers (Section~\ref{sec:var-lagrange}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Refinement keeps the exact geometry.}
\index{isogeometric analysis!refinement}\index{k-refinement@$k$-refinement}\index{knot insertion}%
The three kinds of refinement leave the geometry and its parameterization
unchanged.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{$h$-refinement} is \emph{knot insertion}: each new knot adds one
function, and the new control points are convex combinations of the old
ones.
\item \emph{$p$-refinement} is \emph{degree elevation}; the multiplicity of
each knot is raised by one so that the continuity $C^{p-k}$ is kept.
\item \emph{$k$-refinement} elevates the degree first, then inserts knots.
The new knots carry $C^{p-1}$ continuity, so one obtains high order and high
continuity with very few added degrees of freedom. It has no finite element
counterpart. The two operations do not commute: inserting the knots first
and elevating the degree afterwards keeps the lower continuity at the new
knots, with many more functions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Higher continuity is useful.}
\index{fourth-order problems}\index{Kirchhoff--Love shell}\index{Cahn--Hilliard equation}\index{gradient elasticity}%
A conforming method for a fourth-order problem needs $H^2$, hence $C^1$
functions. $C^1$ or smoother splines discretize such problems directly,
without mixed formulations: Kirchhoff--Love plates and
shells~\cite{KiendlShells2009}, the Cahn--Hilliard phase-field
equation~\cite{GomezCahnHilliard2008}, gradient elasticity.

\paragraph{Accuracy per degree of freedom is better.}
\index{isogeometric analysis!spectral accuracy}%
Smooth splines avoid the ``optical branches'' of the spectrum of higher-order
$C^0$ elements, so vibration and wave problems show much better spectral
accuracy~\cite{CottrellVibrations2006}.

\paragraph{Some difficulties are new.}
\index{trimmed surface}\index{multipatch coupling}\index{local refinement}%
\begin{itemize}
\item CAD describes surfaces, while a solid needs a \emph{volumetric}
parameterization, which must be built.
\item CAD surfaces are often \emph{trimmed}: only part of the parameter
square is used.
\item Complex geometries need \emph{several patches}, which must be coupled.
\item \emph{Local refinement} is less natural, since knot insertion acts on
whole rows of the tensor product. It requires T-splines~\cite{SederbergTsplines2003},
truncated hierarchical B-splines (THB-splines)~\cite{GiannelliTHB2012} or
LR-splines~\cite{DokkenLR2013}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{gbox*}
\textbf{IGA is a Galerkin method like FEM.} It replaces element-local,
interpolatory polynomials by smooth, overlapping spline bases taken from
CAD. Elements survive only as integration cells, and B\'ezier extraction
recovers an element-by-element structure.
\end{gbox*}

\section{Physical interpretation: energies come first}\label{sec:var-phys}
\index{minimal surface}\index{soap film}%
Many equations of physics and geometry \emph{are} Euler--Lagrange equations of
an energy or action~\cite{GilbargTrudinger2001}: the PDE is a consequence of a
minimization principle, not an artificial reformulation.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=0.55cm]
  \node[box] (a) {Energy functional $E[u]$ \ (surface area, elastic energy, \dots)};
  \node[box, below=of a] (b) {Critical point: $\delta E(u) = 0$};
  \node[box, goal, below=of b] (c) {Euler--Lagrange equation (PDE), e.g.\ minimal surface equation};
  \draw[arr] (a) -- (b); \draw[arr] (b) -- (c);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
The area of the graph of $u$ over $\Omega$ is
\[
  A[u]=\int_\Omega\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^2}\dd x .
\]
A soap film spanning a wire frame (the boundary data) minimizes it. The first
variation $\displaystyle \frac{\dd}{\dd t}A[u+t\varphi]\big|_{t=0}=0$ for all test functions
$\varphi$ gives, after integration by parts (Theorem~\ref{thm:ibp}),
\[
  \dive\!\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^2}}\right)=0,
\]
the minimal surface equation~\cite{Courant1950}. The energy comes first
physically; the PDE follows. The same holds for elasticity, where the stored
energy $\tfrac12\eps:\bbC:\eps$ comes first and the Navier equations follow
(Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptic}).

\framepart{Summary}
\begin{gbox*}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Existence} follows from minimizing a coercive, weakly lower
  semicontinuous energy over a reflexive space
  (Section~\ref{sec:var-direct}); without convexity in the gradient a
  minimizing sequence may oscillate forever (Bolza problem); more generally, and without symmetry, from
  the Lax--Milgram theorem (Section~\ref{sec:var-lm}), which covers general
  elliptic operators through G\aa rding's inequality and the Fredholm
  alternative (Section~\ref{sec:var-elliptic}).
  \item \textbf{The weak form} is not an approximation of the strong form
  but a genuine widening of the solution space, made possible by integration
  by parts (Appendix~\ref{app:ibp}).
  \item \textbf{Duality} (Section~\ref{sec:var-dual}) gives every convex
  variational problem a mirror dual problem through the Legendre--Fenchel
  transform; the two meet at the optimum. \textbf{Constraints} enter through
  Lagrange multipliers (Section~\ref{sec:var-lagrange}).
  \item \textbf{The same linear algebra}, $\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}\,\vu=\vf$,
  underlies springs, circuits, trusses (Section~\ref{sec:var-discrete}) and
  finite elements (Section~\ref{sec:var-fem}): compatibility, constitutive
  law and equilibrium, with equilibrium the transpose of compatibility.
  \item \textbf{Galerkin methods project.} The discrete solution is the
  projection of the exact one onto $V_h$ in the energy inner product
  (Galerkin orthogonality, C\'ea's lemma). Finite elements build $V_h$ from
  element-local, interpolatory polynomials, $C^0$ across elements;
  isogeometric analysis builds it from the smooth, overlapping B-splines and
  NURBS of CAD, with the exact geometry (Section~\ref{sec:var-iga}).
  \item \textbf{Not every weak form has an energy.} A symmetric form has
  $J(u)=\tfrac12a(u,u)-L(u)$; a non-symmetric one (convection) has none,
  yet Lax--Milgram still applies.
  \item \textbf{Adjoint operators.} Every problem of the chapter has the
  structure of the Tonti diagram below: a kinematic operator $D$ (the matrix

  and the stiffness operator is always $K=D^\ast CD$: symmetric, and positive
  definite when $C$ is and $D$ has no kernel (no rigid motion).

\framesub{Short questions}

\exercise{One mass, two springs}\label{exo:sq-springs}
A mass is held between two walls by a spring of stiffness $c_1$ on the left
and $c_2$ on the right. Write $\mat{B}$ and $\mat{K}$, and find the
displacement and the spring forces under a force $f$.
\begin{solution}
$e_1=u$, $e_2=-u$, so $\mat{B}=(1,-1)\T$ and
$\mat{K}=\mat{B}\T\mat{C}\mat{B}=c_1+c_2$. Hence $u=f/(c_1+c_2)$,
$\sigma_1=c_1f/(c_1+c_2)$ (tension), $\sigma_2=-c_2f/(c_1+c_2)$ (compression).
Check: $\mat{B}\T\vect{\sigma}=\sigma_1-\sigma_2=f$.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Finite energy or not?}\label{exo:sq-h1}
For which $\alpha>0$ is $u=x^\alpha$ in $H^1(0,1)$? Compute
$\displaystyle \int_0^1(u')^2\dd x$ for $\alpha=\tfrac34$ and discuss
$\alpha=\tfrac13$.
\begin{solution}
$\displaystyle \int_0^1\alpha^2x^{2\alpha-2}\dd x=\frac{\alpha^2}{2\alpha-1}$ if
$\alpha>\tfrac12$, and $+\infty$ otherwise. For $\alpha=\tfrac34$:
$\tfrac{9/16}{1/2}=\tfrac98$. For $\alpha=\tfrac13$ the integral diverges: $x^{1/3}$
is continuous and bounded, yet has infinite energy.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Essential or natural?}\label{exo:sq-bc}
Write the weak form of $-(ku')'=f$ on $(0,1)$, $u(0)=0$, $k(1)u'(1)=g$, with
$k>0$. In which space is it posed? Which condition is essential, which natural?
\begin{solution}
$V=\{v\in H^1(0,1):v(0)=0\}$, and
$\displaystyle \int_0^1ku'v'\dd x=\int_0^1fv\dd x+g\,v(1)$ for all $v\in V$. The
condition $u(0)=0$ is essential (built into $V$); $k(1)u'(1)=g$ is natural (it
appears in the right-hand side, and is recovered by integrating by parts).
\end{solution}

\exercise{A coercivity threshold}\label{exo:sq-coerc}
For which $c\in\R$ is $\displaystyle a(u,v)=\int_0^1u'v'\dd x+c\int_0^1uv\dd x$
coercive on $H^1_0(0,1)$?
\begin{solution}
For $c\ge0$, $\displaystyle a(u,u)\ge\int(u')^2$. For $c<0$, Wirtinger's inequality
$\displaystyle \int u^2\le\frac1{\pi^2}\int(u')^2$ gives
$\displaystyle a(u,u)\ge\Bigl(1+\frac{c}{\pi^2}\Bigr)\int(u')^2$. So $a$ is coercive iff $c>-\pi^2$. At
$c=-\pi^2$, $a(\sin\pi x,\sin\pi x)=0$: resonance (Section~\ref{sec:var-lm}).
\end{solution}

\exercise{The conjugate of a spring energy}\label{exo:sq-lf}
Compute the Legendre--Fenchel transform of $F(e)=\tfrac12ke^2$, $k>0$, and
interpret it.
\begin{solution}
$\sup_e(\sigma e-\tfrac12ke^2)$ is reached at $e=\sigma/k$, so
$F^\ast(\sigma)=\sigma^2/(2k)$: the complementary energy of the spring. The
Fenchel--Young inequality $F(e)+F^\ast(\sigma)\ge\sigma e$ is an equality iff
$\sigma=ke$: Hooke's law.
\end{solution}

\exercise{A reaction from a multiplier}\label{exo:sq-reaction}
A spring of stiffness $c$ links a wall to a mass whose displacement is
imposed, $u=d$. From the Lagrangian $\tfrac12cu^2-\lambda(u-d)$, find $\lambda$
and interpret it.
\begin{solution}
Stationarity: $cu-\lambda=0$ and $u=d$, so $\lambda=cd$. With the minus sign
of~\eqref{eq:lagrangian-eq}, $\lambda$ is the reaction: the support pushes the
mass with the force $cd$ in the direction of $x$, which balances the spring
tension $cd$.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Two linear elements}\label{exo:sq-two-el}
Solve $-u''=1$ on $(0,1)$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$, with two linear elements
($h=\tfrac12$) and the load $\displaystyle F_1=\int_0^1\varphi_1\dd x$. Compare
$u_h(\tfrac12)$ with the exact value.
\begin{solution}
One unknown: $K_{11}=2/h=4$, $F_1=h=\tfrac12$, so $u_h(\tfrac12)=\tfrac18$. The
exact solution $u=\tfrac12x(1-x)$ gives $u(\tfrac12)=\tfrac18$: the nodal value
is exact (Exercise~\ref{exo:sp-nodal}), the error lies between the nodes.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Galerkin orthogonality}\label{exo:sq-orth}
Show that $a(u-u_h,v_h)=0$ for all $v_h\in V_h$. For $a$ symmetric and
coercive, deduce $\norm{u-u_h}_a\le\norm{u-v_h}_a$ for all $v_h\in V_h$.
\begin{solution}
Subtract $a(u_h,v_h)=L(v_h)$ from $a(u,v_h)=L(v_h)$, valid since
$V_h\subset V$. Then, with $w_h=u_h-v_h\in V_h$,
$\norm{u-v_h}_a^2=\norm{u-u_h}_a^2+2a(u-u_h,w_h)+\norm{w_h}_a^2
=\norm{u-u_h}_a^2+\norm{w_h}_a^2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:galerkin-proj}).
\end{solution}

\exercise{Minimizing by gradient descent}\label{exo:gd}
\index{gradient descent}%
For the spring chain ($\mat{K}=\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$,
$\vf=(1,1,1)\T$), minimize $W(\vu)=\tfrac12\vu\T\mat{K}\vu-\vf\T\vu$ by
gradient descent and compare with the direct solution of $\mat{K}\vu=\vf$.
What is the convergence rate?
\begin{solution}
With the step $0.2$ and $500$ iterations, $\vu_n\to(1.5,\,2,\,1.5)\T$, the
direct solution, to machine precision.

\exercise{Convergence rates: finite differences and finite elements}\label{exo:conv}
\index{convergence rate}\index{finite differences}\index{Aubin--Nitsche lemma}%
Solve $-u''=\sin\pi x$ on $(0,1)$, $u(0)=u(1)=0$ (exact solution
$\sin(\pi x)/\pi^2$) with the chain matrix $h^{-1}\operatorname{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$,
for $n=3,7,15,31,63$ interior nodes, and two load vectors.
(a) The \emph{lumped} load $F_i=hf(x_i)$. This is the classical
finite-difference scheme, or equivalently linear finite elements with the
load lumped at the nodes. Measure the maximal nodal error.
(b) The \emph{consistent} load $\displaystyle F_i=\int_0^1f\varphi_i\dd x$, computed
exactly: this is the linear finite element method. Measure the maximal nodal
error, the $L^2$ error and the $H^1$-seminorm error $|u-u_h|_{H^1}$.
Which of these errors does C\'ea's lemma bound?
\begin{solution}
(a) The maximal nodal error is divided by $4$ each time $h$ is halved:
$5.4\times10^{-3}$ for $h=\tfrac14$, $2.0\times10^{-5}$ for $h=\tfrac1{64}$. This
$O(h^2)$ rate comes from the finite-difference analysis: the second
difference approximates $u''$ to $O(h^2)$ (consistency), and the discrete
problem is stable. It is \emph{not} a consequence of C\'ea's lemma, which
bounds the error in the energy norm, $|u-u_h|_{H^1}$, not a maximum over the
nodes.
(b) The nodal error is at machine precision ($\le2\times10^{-15}$): in 1D,
linear elements with the exact load are exact at the nodes, $u_h=I_hu$
(Exercise~\ref{exo:sp-nodal}). The $H^1$-seminorm error is halved with $h$,
$O(h)$: this is what C\'ea's lemma predicts, and it is optimal for
piecewise-linear functions. The $L^2$ error is divided by $4$, $O(h^2)$: this
extra order is the Aubin--Nitsche duality
argument~\cite{Aubin1967,Nitsche1968,Ciarlet2002}, not C\'ea's lemma. Both
rates are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:conv}.
\lstinputlisting[firstline=4]{python/examples/ch1/exo_conv.py}
\end{solution}

\exercise{A non-symmetric bilinear form is still solvable}\label{exo:skew}
Add a skew-symmetric convection matrix $b\,\mat{S}$ to the diffusion matrix
$\mat{K}$. Check that $\vu\T(\mat{K}+b\mat{S})\vu=\vu\T\mat{K}\vu$, that
$\mat{K}+b\mat{S}$ is non-symmetric for $b\neq0$, and that the system remains
uniquely solvable.
\begin{solution}
With the centred-difference matrix $\mat{S}=(\delta_{i+1,j}-\delta_{i-1,j})/(2h)$,
$\mat{S}\T=-\mat{S}$, so $\mat{K}+b\mat{S}$ is non-symmetric for $b\neq0$, while
$\vu\T(\mat{K}+b\mat{S})\vu=\vu\T\mat{K}\vu$ for every $\vu$; the system is
solvable for $b=0,5,50$.
\lstinputlisting[firstline=4]{python/examples/ch1/exo_skew.py} The skew part vanishes on the diagonal $\vu\T\mat{S}\vu=0$, so coercivity is
unaffected: this is why Lax--Milgram applies without symmetry
(Section~\ref{sec:var-elliptic}).
\end{solution}

\exercise{Reciprocity, componentwise}\label{exo:reciprocity}
Split the degrees of freedom of $\vF=\mat{K}\vu$ into two groups $P$ and $Q$,
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}\vF_P\\ \vF_Q\end{bmatrix}=
  \begin{bmatrix}\mat{K}_{PP}&\mat{K}_{PQ}\\ \mat{K}_{QP}&\mat{K}_{QQ}\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}\vu_P\\ \vu_Q\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Load case 1 loads only $P$, load case 2 only $Q$. Write the reciprocity
identity in blocks and show that it holds for all loads iff
$\mat{K}_{QP}=\mat{K}_{PQ}\T$ (with $\mat{K}_{PP}$, $\mat{K}_{QQ}$ symmetric).
Check it numerically on the spring chain with $P=\{1\}$, $Q=\{3\}$.
\begin{solution}
With $\mat{S}=\mat{K}^{-1}$, case 1 gives $\vu^{(1)}=\mat{S}_{\cdot P}\vF_P^{(1)}$
and case 2 gives $\vu^{(2)}=\mat{S}_{\cdot Q}\vF_Q^{(2)}$. Reciprocity
$\vF^{(1)}\cdot\vu^{(2)}=\vF^{(2)}\cdot\vu^{(1)}$ reads
$\vF_P^{(1)}\cdot\mat{S}_{PQ}\vF_Q^{(2)}=\vF_Q^{(2)}\cdot\mat{S}_{QP}\vF_P^{(1)}$
for all loads, i.e.\ $\mat{S}_{QP}=\mat{S}_{PQ}\T$. Taking also $P=Q$ groups
gives the symmetry of the diagonal blocks, so $\mat{S}$, hence $\mat{K}$, is
symmetric. For the chain, $\mat{S}=\tfrac14\begin{psmallmatrix}3&2&1\\2&4&2\\1&2&3\end{psmallmatrix}$:
a unit load at node~1 moves node~3 by $\tfrac14$, and a unit load at node~3
moves node~1 by $\tfrac14$.
\end{solution}

\paragraph{From energy to strong form, through the weak form.}

\exercise{Natural boundary conditions}\label{exo:neumann}
\index{boundary condition!natural}\index{boundary condition!essential}\index{boundary condition!Neumann}\index{compatibility condition}%
On $\Omega=(0,1)$ consider, over the unconstrained space $V=H^1(0,1)$,
\[
  E[u]=\int_0^1\tfrac12(u')^2\dd x-\int_0^1fu\dd x-g\,u(1).
\]
Derive the weak form, then the strong form and the boundary conditions. What
happens at $x=0$, where $E$ has no boundary term? Is the problem well posed?
\begin{solution}
The first variation is
$\displaystyle \delta E(u)[\varphi]=\int_0^1u'\varphi'-\int_0^1f\varphi-g\varphi(1)=0$ for
all $\varphi\in H^1(0,1)$: this is the weak form. Integrating by parts,
$\displaystyle \int_0^1u'\varphi'=-\int_0^1u''\varphi+u'(1)\varphi(1)-u'(0)\varphi(0)$, so
\[
  \int_0^1(-u''-f)\varphi\dd x+\bigl(u'(1)-g\bigr)\varphi(1)-u'(0)\varphi(0)=0
  \quad\forall\varphi .
\]
Interior test functions give $-u''=f$. Then $\varphi(0)$ and $\varphi(1)$ are
arbitrary (nothing forces them to vanish), so $u'(1)=g$ and $u'(0)=0$. Both
conditions come \emph{for free} from the variational principle: they are
\emph{natural}, as opposed to the \emph{essential} condition $u=0$ of the
model problem, which is built into the space $H^1_0$.

Integrating $-u''=f$ and using both conditions gives the compatibility
condition $\displaystyle g=-\int_0^1f\dd x$: a pure Neumann problem is solvable only if the
data satisfy it (Fredholm alternative), and the solution is unique up to a
constant, on which $E$ does not depend (Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, left). A
Ritz check with $u=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2$, $f=1$, $g=-1$: $\partial E/\partial a_0=0$
identically ($a_0$ is free), and the other two conditions give $a_1=0$,
$a_2=-\tfrac12$, i.e.\ $u'=-x$, the exact solution.
\lstinputlisting[firstline=4]{python/examples/ch1/exo_neumann.py}
\end{solution}

\exercise{A reaction term}\label{exo:reaction}
\index{reaction--diffusion}%
Derive the strong form of
$\displaystyle E[u]=\int_\Omega\bigl(\tfrac12|\nabla u|^2+\tfrac12c\,u^2-fu\bigr)\dd x$ over
$H^1_0(\Omega)$, $c>0$ constant.
\begin{solution}
$\displaystyle \delta E(u)[\varphi]=\int_\Omega(\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi+cu\varphi-f\varphi)=0$.
Green's first identity on the first term (no boundary term in $H^1_0$) gives
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega(-\Delta u+cu-f)\varphi=0$ for all $\varphi$, hence
$-\Delta u+cu=f$ in $\Omega$, $u=0$ on $\partial\Omega$. Zeroth-order terms in
the energy become zeroth-order terms in the PDE without integration by
parts. In 1D with $c=2$, $f=x$: $u=x/2-\sinh(\sqrt2x)/(2\sinh\sqrt2)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, middle).
\end{solution}

\exercise{A nonlinear energy}\label{exo:plaplace}
\index{p-Laplacian@$p$-Laplacian}\index{energy!nonlinear}%
Derive the strong form of $\displaystyle E[u]=\int_0^1\tfrac14(u')^4\dd x-\int_0^1fu\dd x$
over $H^1_0(0,1)$.
\begin{solution}
The density is nonlinear in $u'$; differentiate directly:
$\displaystyle \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\tfrac14(u'+t\varphi')^4\big|_{t=0}=(u')^3\varphi'$. Hence
$\displaystyle \delta E(u)[\varphi]=\int_0^1(u')^3\varphi'-\int_0^1f\varphi=0$, and an
integration by parts gives
\[
  -\bigl((u')^3\bigr)'=f\ \text{in }(0,1),\qquad u(0)=u(1)=0 .
\]
This is the one-dimensional $p$-Laplacian, $-(|u'|^{p-2}u')'=f$ with $p=4$; in
$\R^n$, $-\dive(|\nabla u|^2\nabla u)=f$. The energy penalizes large slopes
more strongly than the quadratic one, which flattens the profile
(Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}, right).
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Guessing the energy from a weak form.}

\exercise{Reconstruct the energy}\label{exo:guess-energy}
\index{energy!from a weak form}%
Find $J$ whose first variation is the weak form: find $u\in H^1(\Omega)$ with
\[
  \int_\Omega\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\dd x+\int_\Omega\sigma uv\dd x
  =\int_\Omega fv\dd x+\int_{\partial\Omega}gv\dd s\qquad\forall v\in H^1(\Omega),
\]
$\kappa,\sigma>0$ constants.
\begin{solution}
If $a(u,v)=L(v)$ with $a$ \textbf{symmetric} bilinear and $L$ linear, then
$J(u)=\tfrac12a(u,u)-L(u)$ has $\delta J(u)[v]=\tfrac12\bigl(a(u,v)+a(v,u)\bigr)-L(v)=a(u,v)-L(v)$.
Here $a$ is symmetric, so
\[
  J(u)=\tfrac12\int_\Omega\kappa|\nabla u|^2+\tfrac12\int_\Omega\sigma u^2
  -\int_\Omega fu-\int_{\partial\Omega}gu .
\]
\end{solution}

\exercise{When no energy exists}\label{exo:no-energy}
\index{energy!non-existence}%
For the convection--diffusion weak form
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\int_\Omega(\vb\cdot\nabla u)v=\int_\Omega fv$
on $H^1_0(\Omega)$, $\vb\neq\vzero$ constant, is there an energy $J$ with
$\delta J(u)[v]=a(u,v)-L(v)$?
\begin{solution}
If $J$ existed, its second derivative $a$ would be symmetric (Schwarz's
theorem on mixed derivatives). But for $u,v\in H^1_0$ and constant $\vb$,
integration by parts gives
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega(\vb\cdot\nabla u)v=-\int_\Omega(\vb\cdot\nabla v)u$: the convection
part is \emph{anti}symmetric, so $a(u,v)\neq a(v,u)$ in general. No energy
exists, while Lax--Milgram applies (Section~\ref{sec:var-elliptic}). In 1D
with $b=3$, $u=\sin\pi x$, $v=\sin2\pi x$: $a(u,v)=4$, $a(v,u)=-4$. The skew
part vanishes only on the diagonal $a(u,u)$ (Exercise~\ref{exo:skew}).
\end{solution}

\paragraph{Integration by parts in practice.}

\exercise{Self-adjointness}\label{exo:selfadj}
\index{self-adjointness}%
Let $u,v\in H^2(\Omega)\cap H^1_0(\Omega)$. Use Green's second identity to show
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega u\Delta v=\int_\Omega v\Delta u$.
\begin{solution}
Theorem~\ref{thm:green2} gives
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega(u\Delta v-v\Delta u)=\int_{\partial\Omega}(u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu)$,
and every boundary term contains a vanishing factor $u$ or $v$. This
self-adjointness of the Dirichlet Laplacian is what makes $a(u,v)$ symmetric
and the minimization approach possible.
\end{solution}

\exercise{The Robin problem}\label{exo:robin}
\index{boundary condition!Robin}%
Derive the weak form of $-\Delta u=f$ in $\Omega$, $\partial_nu+\alpha u=g$ on
$\partial\Omega$ ($\alpha>0$), and its energy.
\begin{solution}
Multiply by $v\in H^1(\Omega)$ (Robin, like Neumann, is natural) and use
Green's first identity:
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v-\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_nu\,v=\int_\Omega fv$.
Substituting $\partial_nu=g-\alpha u$:
\[
  \int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}uv
  =\int_\Omega fv+\int_{\partial\Omega}gv\qquad\forall v\in H^1(\Omega).
\]
The form is symmetric, so
$\displaystyle J(u)=\tfrac12\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2+\tfrac\alpha2\int_{\partial\Omega}u^2-\int_\Omega fu-\int_{\partial\Omega}gu$.
The Robin condition adds a boundary ``spring'' term, between Dirichlet
($\alpha\to\infty$) and Neumann ($\alpha=0$).
\end{solution}

\exercise{Dirichlet energy from boundary data}\label{exo:dirichlet-energy}
Let $\Delta u=0$ in $\Omega$. Show that
$\displaystyle \int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2=\int_{\partial\Omega}u\,\partial_nu$, and check it on
$(0,1)^3$ for $u=x^2+y^2-2z^2$.
\begin{solution}
Take $v=u$ in Green's first identity; the volume term $\displaystyle \int u\Delta u$
vanishes. The energy of a harmonic field is determined by its boundary data:
this identity is used to compute capacities, and in Chapter~\ref{ch:rg} the
same idea turns boundary measurements into information on a crack. For
$u=x^2+y^2-2z^2$: $\displaystyle \int|\nabla u|^2=\int(4x^2+4y^2+16z^2)=8$; on the boundary, the faces
$x=1$, $y=1$, $z=1$ give $\tfrac43$, $\tfrac43$, $\tfrac{16}3$ and the others $0$: total $8$.
\end{solution}

\framesub{Part III: constraints and convex conjugates}

\exercise{An equality constraint}\label{exo:kkt1}
Minimize $C(\vx)=\tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ subject to $x_1-x_2=1$.
\begin{solution}
With the convention~\eqref{eq:lagrangian-eq},
$\mathcal L=\tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)-\lambda(x_1-x_2-1)$. Stationarity:
$x_1-\lambda=0$, $x_2+\lambda=0$; the constraint gives $2\lambda=1$. Hence
$\lambda=\tfrac12$, $\vx=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$, $C=\tfrac14$: the point of the
line closest to the origin. With this sign the multiplier is also the
sensitivity of the optimum to the data: for the constraint $x_1-x_2=b$,
$C_{\min}(b)=b^2/4$ and $\dd C_{\min}/\dd b=\tfrac12=\lambda$ at $b=1$.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Two constraints}\label{exo:kkt2}
Minimize $C(\vx)=\tfrac12|\vx|^2$ in $\R^3$ subject to $x_1-x_2=1$ and
$x_2-x_3=2$.
\begin{solution}
Write the constraints $\mat{P}\vx=\vb$ with
$\mat{P}=\begin{psmallmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\end{psmallmatrix}$, $\vb=(1,2)$.
With $\mathcal L=\tfrac12|\vx|^2-\vlambda\cdot(\mat{P}\vx-\vb)$, stationarity
gives $\vx=\mat{P}\T\vlambda$, and the constraints
$\mat{P}\mat{P}\T\vlambda=\vb$ with
$\mat{P}\mat{P}\T=\begin{psmallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{psmallmatrix}$. Hence
$\vlambda=(\tfrac43,\tfrac53)$ and $\vx=(\tfrac43,\tfrac13,-\tfrac53)$;
check: $x_1-x_2=1$, $x_2-x_3=2$.
\end{solution}

\exercise{A quadratic form on the circle}\label{exo:kkt3}
\index{eigenvalue problem!as constrained minimization}%
Minimize $C(\vx)=ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+cx_2^2$ subject to $x_1^2+x_2^2=1$.
\begin{solution}
With $\mat{H}=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{psmallmatrix}$,
$\mathcal L=\vx\T\mat{H}\vx-\lambda(|\vx|^2-1)$ gives $\mat{H}\vx=\lambda\vx$:
the stationary points are the unit eigenvectors, and $C=\lambda$ there. The
minimum is the smallest eigenvalue,
$\tfrac{a+c}2-\sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{a-c}2\bigr)^2+b^2}$. Constrained minimization
of a quadratic form is an eigenvalue problem: this is the root of the
Rayleigh quotient, of~\eqref{eq:rayleigh}, and of the buckling problems later
in the course.
\end{solution}

\exercise{The saddle-point system}\label{exo:kkt4}
\index{saddle point!system}%
Minimize $C(\vx)=\tfrac12\vx\T\mat{H}\vx-\vx\T\vf$, $\mat{H}$ symmetric
positive definite, subject to $\mat{P}\vx=\vb$ ($\mat{P}$ of full row rank).
\begin{solution}
$\mathcal L=C(\vx)-\vlambda\cdot(\mat{P}\vx-\vb)$, as
in~\eqref{eq:lagrangian-eq}, and stationarity in $(\vx,\vlambda)$ gives
$\mat{H}\vx=\vf+\mat{P}\T\vlambda$ and $\mat{P}\vx=\vb$, that is
\[
  \begin{bmatrix}\mat{H}&-\mat{P}\T\\ -\mat{P}&\mat{0}\end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix}\vx\\ \vlambda\end{bmatrix}=
  \begin{bmatrix}\vf\\ -\vb\end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \vlambda=(\mat{P}\mat{H}^{-1}\mat{P}\T)^{-1}(\vb-\mat{P}\mat{H}^{-1}\vf),\quad
  \vx=\mat{H}^{-1}(\vf+\mat{P}\T\vlambda).
\]
The matrix is symmetric but indefinite: $(\vx,\vlambda)$ is a saddle point,
a minimum in $\vx$ and a maximum in $\vlambda$. In mechanics ($\mat{H}$ a
stiffness, $\vf$ the loads, $\mat{P}\vx=\vb$ imposed displacements), the
first line is equilibrium with the reactions $\vect{r}=\mat{P}\T\vlambda$, as
in~\eqref{eq:saddle-springs}. This is the system used for imposed
displacements in Chapter~\ref{ch:elliptic}.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Legendre--Fenchel transforms}\label{exo:lf}
(a) Give a geometric interpretation of $F^\ast$ in $\R^2$ and prove the
Fenchel--Young inequality. (b) Compute $F^\ast$ for $F(x)=x^2$, $F(x)=x^4$,
$F(x)=e^x$, and the indicator $F(x)=0$ for $x\le0$, $+\infty$ for $x>0$.
\begin{solution}
(a) $F^\ast(p)$ is the largest gap between the line $px$ and the graph of
$F$; the line $px-F^\ast(p)$ is the supporting line of slope $p$
(Figure~\ref{fig:legendre}). By definition $F^\ast(p)\ge px-F(x)$ for every
$x$, which is Fenchel--Young; equality holds where the supremum is attained,
$p=F'(x)$.
(b) $x^2$: the maximum of $px-x^2$ is at $x=p/2$, $F^\ast(p)=p^2/4$.
$x^4$: $p=4x^3$, $F^\ast(p)=3(p/4)^{4/3}$.
$e^x$: $F^\ast(p)=p\ln p-p$ for $p>0$, $0$ for $p=0$, $+\infty$ for $p<0$.
Indicator of $\{x\le0\}$: $F^\ast(p)=\sup_{x\le0}px$, which is $0$ for $p\ge0$
and $+\infty$ for $p<0$, the indicator of $\{p\ge0\}$. This last pair is the
convex-analysis form of the complementarity conditions of
Section~\ref{sec:var-lagrange}, and reappears in plasticity.
\end{solution}

\exercise{Quadratic B-splines by hand}\label{exo:bsplines}
\index{B-spline!exercise}\index{Bezier extraction@B\'ezier extraction!exercise}%
Take $p=2$ and the open knot vector $\Xi=\{0,0,0,1,2,3,3,3\}$.
(a) How many basis functions and how many elements are there?
(b) Compute the $N_{i,2}$ with the Cox--de Boor recursion, span by span.
(c) Check the partition of unity and the continuity at the interior knots
$\xi=1$ and $\xi=2$: are $N_{i,2}$, $N_{i,2}'$, $N_{i,2}''$ continuous?
(d) Which functions are interpolatory at the ends?
(e) Find the B\'ezier extraction operator $\mat{C}^e$ of the element $[1,2]$.
\begin{solution}
(a) $n=8-p-1=5$ functions on $3$ elements $[0,1]$, $[1,2]$, $[2,3]$.
(b) The only nonzero degree-0 functions are $N_{3,0}$, $N_{4,0}$, $N_{5,0}$,
equal to $1$ on $[0,1)$, $[1,2)$, $[2,3)$. Two steps of the recursion give,
on the three spans,
\[
\begin{array}{llll}
 & [0,1] & [1,2] & [2,3]\\[2pt]
N_1 & (1-\xi)^2 & 0 & 0\\
N_2 & \tfrac12\xi(4-3\xi) & \tfrac12(2-\xi)^2 & 0\\
N_3 & \tfrac12\xi^2 & \tfrac12(-2\xi^2+6\xi-3) & \tfrac12(3-\xi)^2\\
N_4 & 0 & \tfrac12(\xi-1)^2 & \tfrac12(3-\xi)(3\xi-5)\\
N_5 & 0 & 0 & (\xi-2)^2
\end{array}
\]
(c) On each span the five pieces sum to $1$. At $\xi=1$ and $\xi=2$ the values
and the first derivatives match (for instance $N_2(1)=\tfrac12$,
$N_2'(1)=-1$ from both sides), while the second derivatives jump: the
functions are $C^1=C^{p-1}$, as expected for simple knots.
(d) $N_1(0)=1$ and $N_5(3)=1$, all others vanish at the ends: the end
functions are interpolatory, the interior control variables are not values.
(e) With $t=\xi-1$ and the Bernstein polynomials
$\mat{b}=\bigl((1-t)^2,\,2t(1-t),\,t^2\bigr)$, the active functions $N_2$, $N_3$,
$N_4$ on $[1,2]$ are
$\mat{N}^e=\mat{C}^e\mat{b}$ with
$\mat{C}^e=\begin{psmallmatrix}1/2&0&0\\1/2&1&1/2\\0&0&1/2\end{psmallmatrix}$.
The same computation in \texttt{sympy}:
\lstinputlisting[firstline=4]{python/examples/ch1/exo_bsplines.py}
\end{solution}

\exercise{Counting degrees of freedom}\label{exo:dof-count}
\index{isogeometric analysis!degrees of freedom}%
A cube is meshed with $n_{el}$ elements per direction. Count the unknowns of
a scalar field, without boundary conditions, (a) for Lagrange elements of
degree $p$, (b) for B-splines of degree $p$ with maximal continuity on an
open knot vector. Give the numbers for $n_{el}=10$ and $p=2,3$. (c) Which knot
vector gives back the finite element space?
\begin{solution}
In one direction: (a) $p$ new nodes per element plus the first one,
$p\,n_{el}+1$; (b) the open knot vector has $2(p+1)+(n_{el}-1)$ knots, hence
$n_{el}+p$ functions. In 3D the counts are cubed:
\[
\begin{array}{lcc}
 & \text{Lagrange, }(p\,n_{el}+1)^3 & \text{B-splines, }(n_{el}+p)^3\\[2pt]
p=2 & 21^3=9261 & 12^3=1728\\
p=3 & 31^3=29791 & 13^3=2197
\end{array}
\]
For a fine mesh the ratio tends to $p^3$: smooth splines reach a given degree
with far fewer unknowns, but each function overlaps more elements, so the
matrix is less sparse. (c) Repeating each interior knot $p$ times gives $C^0$
splines and $2(p+1)+p(n_{el}-1)-p-1=p\,n_{el}+1$ functions: the space of
continuous piecewise polynomials of degree $p$, the same space as Lagrange
elements, in the Bernstein basis instead of the nodal one.
\end{solution}
